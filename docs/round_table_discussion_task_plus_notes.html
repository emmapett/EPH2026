Round table discussions: machine learning in public health research

Round table discussions

Select one topic for your group to discuss.

No technical knowledge of machine learning (ML) is needed. You can draw on concepts from epidemiology and public health, such as bias, confounding, validity and causal inference, as well as your own expertise and experience from your field.

Time Activity
5 min Form tables (5 groups, 5-6 people per group), appoint a note-taker, read the guiding questions and scenario
20 min Discussion
20 min Each table reports their main takeaways (3 min per group), followed by questions or comments from the plenary

The guiding questions and scenarios are meant to be prompts for the round-table discussion. Feel free to raise related questions or examples from your own experience, and to move on to other topics if time allows.

Topic 1: Prediction, explanation or causation?

Guiding questions

  1. How would you define prediction, explanation and causation in your own words?
  2. Can a strong predictor be non-causal? Can a cause be a weak predictor? Give examples from your own field.
  3. Do the three aims require different study designs, analyses and interpretation?
NoteNotes: guiding questions

  1. Definitions

    • Prediction: estimating an unobserved outcome from available information. It does not require knowing why the prediction works.
    • Explanation: describing and understanding how factors relate to an outcome (mechanisms, associations).
    • Causation: a relationship where changing or intervening on a cause (X) produces a direct, predictable change in an outcome (Y).

  2. Predictors and causes

    • A strong predictor is not always a cause. It may be an early sign of disease, a marker of treatment, or a stand-in for something else, such as where people live or how often they use healthcare.
    • A cause is not always a strong predictor. For example, if an exposure is rare, has a small effect, or is something everyone shares (no variation to predict from)

  3. Designs and interpretation

    • Prediction: representative data from the target population, predictors available at the time of use, and validation (discrimination, calibration). Any variable that helps is allowed, including markers.
    • Causal: a defined exposure and comparison, confounder control guided by subject knowledge (not by what improves fit), and designs such as trials or target trial emulation.
    • Interpretation: coefficients or variable importance from a prediction model are not effects (the “Table 2 fallacy”).
      • A prediction model includes whatever variables improve prediction, so each coefficient might be adjusted for all the others, including variables that lie on the causal pathway (mediators) or that are consequences of the outcome. A causal estimate needs a separate set of adjustments for each exposure, chosen from subject knowledge.
      • A high variable importance score only says the model used that variable. It does not say that changing it would change the outcome.

Scenarios

  1. The protective paradox. A hospital model predicting death from pneumonia learns that patients with asthma have lower risk than others. In reality, they were sent straight to intensive care and treated aggressively.
  2. Signal or cause? A register study finds that a medication is an important predictor of dementia in a machine learning model.
  3. The diabetes model. A machine learning model predicts type 2 diabetes within ten years very accurately. The strongest predictors are age, BMI, recent blood sugar level, statin use and low physical activity.
NoteNotes: scenarios
  • (A) The protective paradox. The model learned the pattern in a setting where asthma patients received aggressive care. It predicts well there, but using it to decide who needs intensive care would harm patients. A model can find patterns without knowing what produces it. To use a prediction models effectively, we need to understand the mechanisms behind it.
  • (B) Signal or cause? Possible explanations: a real causal effect, reverse causation (the drug was prescribed for early symptoms such as sleep problems, anxiety or depression), confounding by indication, or healthcare use. An important predictor in an ML model is a signal worth investigating, not evidence of a cause.
  • (C) The diabetes model. A model like this could be good for targeting screening, but may not tell you what to intervene on:
    • Age: risk factor, not modifiable.
    • BMI and physical activity: possible causes, but entangled with other things.
    • Blood sugar: a marker of early disease.
    • Statins: mainly a marker of cardiometabolic risk, although trials suggest a small causal increase in diabetes risk.

Topic 2: Evaluating model performance

Guiding questions

  1. What is a ‘good’ prediction model?
  2. What criteria should we apply when evaluating a study reporting a prediction model?
  3. A model claims to be “95% accurate”. What does that tell us, and what does it leave out?
  4. Why is validation in new data, different settings and different time periods important?
NoteNotes: guiding questions

  1. A “good” model is good for a purpose. It should be:

    • accurate and well calibrated in the people it will be used on (calibrated means the predicted risks match what actually happens, so “20% risk” means about 1 in 5 people get the outcome),
    • validated outside the data it was built on (tested on new people, to check it works beyond the original sample),
    • better than a simple alternative or current practice (otherwise, why use it?).
    • It should also support real decisions (net benefit: does acting on the model do more good than harm?) and be fair and transparent enough for how it will be used.

  2. Evaluating models

    • Clear intended use and target population, with a well-defined outcome and predictors available at the time of use.
    • Enough events for the number of predictors. (With few events and many predictors, the model starts fitting noise instead of real patterns.)
    • No data leakage. (Information that would not be available at prediction time has slipped in, for example a treatment given only after diagnosis, which makes the model look better than it is.)
    • Sensible handling of missing data. (Dropping everyone with a missing value can bias the results)
    • Internal and external validation. (Internal: testing on new samples drawn from the same data, for example by splitting or resampling. External: testing in a different place, population or time period, which is the tougher and more honest test.)
    • Both discrimination and calibration reported. (Discrimination: does the model rank people with the outcome above those without? Calibration: do the predicted risks match what actually happens? A model can be good at one and poor at the other.)
    • A comparison with a simple baseline, performance by subgroup, and transparent reporting (TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST).

  3. “95% accurate” depends on how common the outcome is. If 5% have the disease, a model that predicts “healthy” for everyone is 95% accurate but flags nobody. It leaves out:

    • Sensitivity (of those with the disease, how many are found?) and specificity (of those without, how many are correctly cleared?).
    • Predictive values (when the model says “positive”, how often is it right? This depends on how common the disease is.)
    • Calibration and the threshold used (the cut-off for “high risk”, which sets the balance of missed cases and false alarms).
    • Which data the result comes from: the data the model trained on or new data

  4. Why validation matters

    • Performance on the data the model was trained on is often optimistic (overfitting: the model has partly memorised noise in that dataset, which will not repeat in new people).
    • Case mix (who the patients are), measurement (how things are recorded), practice patterns (how care is delivered) and time all change. A model must therefore be tested where and when it will be used, and checked again over time.

Scenarios

  1. The 95% accurate model. A paper reports that its model for detecting a rare disease is “95% accurate”. Only 5% of the population have the disease.

  2. Same model, new hospital. A model predicting hospital admission worked very well where it was developed. In a hospital in another region, performance drops sharply.

NoteNotes: scenarios
  • (A) The 95% accurate model. Imagine 1,000 people where 5% have the disease: 50 are ill and 950 are healthy. A model that simply says “healthy” for everyone would be 95% accurate, yet it would flag nobody. Even a model that correctly finds 90% of the ill people (sensitivity) and correctly clears 90% of the healthy people (specificity) finds 45 of the 50 ill people but also wrongly flags 95 healthy ones. Of the 140 people it flags, only 45 (about 1 in 3) are actually ill. Accuracy alone can hide these problems, so ask how many cases the model finds, how many false alarms it raises, and whether its predicted risks are right (calibration).
  • (B) Same model, new hospital. Likely reasons the model works worse in the new hospital:
    • Different case mix - the patients are different (age, severity, other conditions).
    • Things are measured or recorded differently.
    • Patients are referred and treated differently.
    • The model had partly learned quirks of the original hospital (overfitting).

Topic 3: ML versus conventional epidemiological methods

Guiding questions

  1. When, if ever, do flexible ML methods offer a real advantage over regression?
  2. What does a model gain, and lose, in interpretability and transparency?
  3. Are published claims of superiority reliable? What would make you trust or doubt them?
NoteNotes: guiding questions

  1. When ML helps

    • Unstructured data (images, free text, sensor signals, genomics), very large datasets, and strong non-linear or complex patterns.
    • For structured, tabular data with a moderate sample size and a noisy outcome, which is typical in epidemiology, gains are often small. A well-known systematic review found no consistent advantage over logistic regression for clinical prediction.
    • Flexible methods generally need more data.

  2. Gain and loss

    • Gain: flexibility, automatic handling of interactions and non-linearity, and the ability to use raw unstructured data.
    • Loss: harder to understand, audit and check for bias, with more tuning and more data needed.
    • Post hoc “explanations” approximate what the model does and do not explain the world.

  3. Trust or doubt

    • Reasons to doubt a claim that ML is better:
      • A weak or untuned comparison (the regression was given only a few variables and no tuning, so it was set up to lose).
      • The two methods were treated differently (different data, different cleaning, different tests).
      • Tuning on the test data. (The model was adjusted until it looked good on the same data used to judge it, which inflates the result.)
      • Only one number reported, often AUC (a summary of how well the model ranks people), with no calibration.
      • No external validation (no test in a different place or time period).
    • Reasons to trust it:
      • The comparison was planned in advance (pre-specified), not chosen after seeing the results.
      • Same data and same validation for every method.
      • A strong baseline (a well-built regression, or current practice).
      • External validation, calibration and various performance metrics compared and reported.

Scenarios

  1. The small gain. Two teams analyse the same dataset. One uses logistic regression, the other a complex ML method. The ML method performs slightly better.

  2. The unfair comparison. A paper claims its new ML method is “far better than conventional methods”. The regression it was compared with used only age and sex and was not tuned.

  3. Why was this flagged? A hospital considers a complex model for flagging patients at risk of deterioration. A clinician asks why a particular patient was flagged, and nobody can say.

  4. Images and free text. A group wants to predict an outcome from chest X-rays and free-text clinical notes.

NoteNotes: scenarios
  • (A) The small gain. Is the difference ‘real’, larger than the uncertainty (confidence intervals), and does it change any decision? Prefer the simpler model unless the gain is clearly worth it.
    • Also remember that different models can be good at different things: one may be better at ranking people, another at giving accurate risks, so look at more than one performance measure
  • (B) The unfair comparison. The claim is not reliable, because the regression was set up to lose. A fair comparison would give the regression the same predictors as the ML method, allow non-linear (curved) relationships and interactions (or use penalisation, which stops the model from overfitting), apply the same validation, and compare both against current practice.
  • (C) Why was this flagged? Clinicians need to be able to question a flag before acting on it. Tools that explain complex models give partial insight, but is not the same as showing what causes the outcome. Ask whether a simpler, more transparent model would do nearly as well. If it would, the extra complexity may not be worth it.
  • (D) Images and free text. This is where flexible methods genuinely help, because regression cannot use raw pixels or free text directly. Some concerns remain:
    • Different scanners and hospitals can produce different images, so performance may drop elsewhere.
    • Label quality: the model is only as good as the diagnoses it learned from.
    • Shortcuts: models can latch onto irrelevant clues (for example, a hospital marker on the scan) instead of the disease.

Topic 4: Ethics and equity

Guiding questions

  1. How should fairness, privacy and accountability be assessed before a model is used in public health practice?
  2. Can a model be accurate overall and still harmful for some groups?
  3. Who decides what is an acceptable error, and who bears the consequences?
NoteNotes: guiding questions

  1. Assessment before use

    • Purpose and benefit: who benefits, who could be harmed, and is there evidence that it improves outcomes?
    • Fairness: performance (discrimination, calibration, error rates) by subgroup (for example by sex, migrant status, age), and whether the outcome predicted is a fair stand-in for what we care about (for example, healthcare costs are not the same as health needs).
    • Privacy: Is there a legal basis for using the data? Is only the necessary data used (data minimisation)? Could individuals be identified again from the data (re-identification risk)? Is it stored and handled securely (e.g., under GDPR)?
    • Accountability: Is it clear who is responsible if something goes wrong? Is there human oversight (a person who can overrule the model)? Can people question or contest a prediction about them? Is the model monitored after it is put into use?

  2. Accurate overall, harmful for some

    • Yes. A high overall accuracy can hide poor performance in groups that are under-represented in the data. The data the model is trained on from can also be biased (for example, if some groups are diagnosed later or less often, the model learns that they are healthier than they are). Proxies (stand-in variables, such as postcode or healthcare use) can also carry existing inequality into the model.
    • A model can also be equally accurate across groups and still cause harm through how it is used (stigma, denial of resources).
    • When base rates differ between groups, different fairness criteria cannot all be satisfied at once (for example, you cannot always have both equal error rates and equally accurate risk estimates across groups), so trade-offs are unavoidable.

  3. Acceptable error

    • How many false positives or false negatives are tolerable is a value judgement, not only a technical choice. Clinicians, public health professionals, patients and affected communities should all have a say.
    • The costs often fall on those with least power: false positives bring anxiety and stigma, false negatives mean missed care.

Scenarios

  1. Who is invited first? A health service uses a prediction model to decide who is invited first to a prevention programme. Places are limited.

  2. Good overall, worse for some. A model has high accuracy overall but is much less accurate for women, older adults or an ethnic minority.

  3. A label that sticks. A model flags a person as high risk of a stigmatised condition. The result is shared with other services. The prediction turns out to be wrong.

NoteNotes: scenarios
  • (A) Who is invited first? High predicted risk is not the same as high expected benefit. The people most at risk are not always the ones who would gain most from the programme.
    • People with sparse records may be invisible to the model, because it only knows what has been recorded about them (for example, people who rarely see a doctor).
    • People already in contact with services may be favoured, because they have more data and so more reliable predictions.
  • (B) Good overall, worse for some.
    • Ask why (imbalanced training data, differences in measurement, different disease presentation). O
    • Options include collecting more representative data, adjusting the model for each group (group-specific calibration, so risks are right in every group, or group-specific thresholds, different cut-offs for calling someone “high risk”), adding human review, or not using the model for that group. Each option has trade-offs, so it needs to be discussed openly.
  • (C) A label that sticks. Being flagged can follow a person around
    • Purpose and consent: Should the result be shared with other services at all, and did the person agree? (Purpose limitation: data should only be used for the reason it was collected.)
    • Right to correct or contest: Can the person see the flag and challenge it if it is wrong?
      • In the US, opioid risk scores (e.g., NarxCare) shown to doctors and pharmacists have reportedly led to patients being refused pain medication or dropped by clinics, often without the patient knowing the score existed or how it was calculated.
      • In Australia, the “Robodebt” scheme (2015-2019) automatically raised debts against welfare recipients, who then had to prove they did not owe the money. It was ruled unlawful and examined by a Royal Commission in 2023.
    • Wording: Present the result as a probability (“higher chance of…”) and not as a diagnosis (“has…”).
    • Time limits: Should the flag expire or be reviewed, so a wrong or outdated prediction does not stay on someone’s record?
      • The London Metropolitan Police “Gangs Matrix” listed thousands of mostly young men as at risk of gang violence. A 2018 review by the UK data regulator found problems with how long people stayed on the list and how widely the information was shared with other agencies, and the list was overhauled.

Topic 5: Bias and representativeness in big data

Guiding questions

  1. Who is likely to be missing, or poorly measured, in data that was not collected for research (e.g., health records, apps, social media)?
    • How could selection bias and measurement error affect what a model learns, and who it works for?
  2. How much data do you need for machine learning? What does the answer depend on?
  3. Does having more data reduce bias, or can it make it worse?
  4. What would you want to know about the data before trusting a model built on it?
NoteNotes: guiding questions

  1. Who is missing or poorly measured

    • People who do not use healthcare or apps: the healthy, young people, migrants, rural or digitally excluded groups, and people with limited access.
    • Records exist only when there is contact with the health service, so the data reflect who is seen as well as who is ill.
    • Effect on the model: selection bias means the model learns relationships in the selected group, which may not hold elsewhere. Differences in recording mean the model can learn diagnosis behaviour rather than disease, and perform worse where data are noisier.

  2. How much data

    • There is no single number. It depends mainly on the number of outcome events (not total sample size), the number of candidate predictors, the strength of the predictor-outcome relationships, and how flexible the method is.
    • Old rules such as “10 events per variable” are crude; formal sample size calculations for prediction models now exist. With few events, simpler methods are safer.

  3. More data and bias

    • More data reduces random error but not systematic error. A very large biased sample can give a precise answer to the wrong question.
      • The bias-variance tradeoff. Prediction error on new data has three parts: bias, variance and irreducible noise (randomness no model can remove).
      • Bias: the model is too simple and misses real patterns (underfitting). It stays wrong however much data you add.
      • Variance: the model is too flexible and fits random noise (overfitting). It changes a lot between samples and does worse on new data.
      • The tradeoff: more complexity lowers bias but raises variance. The best model balances the two.
    • More diverse and representative data can reduce bias.

  4. Before trusting a model

    • How and why the data were collected, who is in the data and who is not, and how the outcome was defined and measured.
    • Missing data, changes in coding/measurement over time, linkage quality, and how the development population compares with the population of use.

Scenarios

  1. A million people, few cases. A register contains 1,000,000 people, but only 300 develop the rare outcome of interest. Is this “big data”?

  2. Many variables, few people. A group has 500 biomarkers measured in 200 participants and wants to use a complex ML method.

  3. Moving to a new population. A model developed in one hospital is proposed for use in a region with a different age, ethnic and socioeconomic profile.

  4. Depression prediction. A model is trained on electronic health records and app data to predict depression in the population.

NoteNotes: scenarios

  • (A) A million people, few cases. A big dataset does not always contain a lot of information. What matters more is how many people actually have the outcome. With only 300 cases, the model has relatively little to learn from, however many people are in the register. How much data is enough also depends on the data itself. If the link between the predictors and the outcome is strong and clear, a modest number of cases can be enough. If the outcome is hard to predict, or people vary a lot in ways the data do not capture, many more cases are needed to find a reliable pattern.

  • (B) Many variables, few people. With hundreds of measurements on only a few people, a complex model can end up memorising the data (overfitting) instead of finding real patterns. The model may look good in the original data but fail on new people.

  • (C) Moving to a new population. A model learns from the people it was built on. If the new region has different people, different health patterns or different ways of recording care, the predictions may no longer fit.

  • (D) Depression prediction. Someone only appears as “depressed” in the records if they sought help and were diagnosed, and that differs between groups. The model may learn who gets diagnosed rather than who is depressed. App users are also a self-selected group.
